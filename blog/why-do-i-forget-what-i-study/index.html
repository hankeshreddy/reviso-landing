Why do I forget what I study?

Padha hua yaad kyun nahi rehta? Follow these 3 rules, in this order.

Reviso team4 Nov 20264 min read

⚡ Short answer

You forget for three reasons: you studied while distracted, you did not revise at the right time, and you never tested yourself. Fix them in order with 3 rules: 1) Respect your study time (focus, phone away), 2) Revise at the right time (spaced repetition), and 3) Test yourself (active recall with MCQs and flashcards). Bonus: keep a short summary of every chapter and get hard topics explained simply.

Forgetting is normal. Losing marks to it is not.

Your brain is built to drop things it does not use. Without review, memory fades fast in the first days, which is the "forgetting curve" Hermann Ebbinghaus described over a hundred years ago. The good news: three habits, done in the right order, stop most of that loss. Each rule fixes a different leak.

Rule 1: Respect your study time

The leak: you sit for two hours, but with the phone next to you, you really study for forty minutes. Distracted study never goes in properly, so there is nothing to remember later.

  • Decide before you start: one chapter or one hour, your choice. Once you decide, finish it. After that, do whatever you want.
  • Work in focus blocks: 25 minutes of study, 5 minutes of break (the Pomodoro method).
  • Keep the phone far away. Even a silent phone on the desk pulls on your attention (Ward and colleagues, 2017).

Rule 2: Revise at the right time

The leak: you finish a chapter, move on, and see it again only before the exam. By then most of it has faded.

  • Revise in growing gaps: after about 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 14 days and 30 days.
  • Keep each revision short: 10 minutes is enough when it is on time.
  • Spreading practice out like this leads to far better long-term memory than one big session (Cepeda and colleagues, 2006).

Rule 3: Test yourself (active recall)

The leak: you revise by re-reading. It feels familiar, so it feels learned, but the exam asks you to pull the answer out with the book closed.

  • Ask yourself questions, again and again. MCQs, flashcards, or writing answers on a blank page.
  • Track what you get wrong. Those are your weak points; revise them first.
  • Students who practise recall remember much more a week later than students who spend the same time re-reading (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Jitni practice, utna pakka.

Bonus: summaries and simple explanations

Two small habits make all three rules easier:

  • Keep a 5-line summary of every chapter. It makes quick revision possible on busy days and the night before the exam.
  • Never memorise what you do not understand. If a topic feels hard, get it explained in simple words with an example first. Understood material is far easier to remember than rote-learned lines.

Questions students ask

🤔Why do I forget everything I studied a few days ago?

Without review, memory fades fastest in the first few days. Revising on a spaced schedule and testing yourself each time slows that fading a lot.

⏳Which rule is the most important?

Rule 3, testing yourself, makes the biggest difference for exams. But it works best on top of focused study (Rule 1) and timely revision (Rule 2).

🗣️How much time do these rules take every day?

Your normal study time, plus about 10 to 20 minutes for revisions and quick tests.

🎯Should I make my own summaries?

Yes, a short summary per chapter helps a lot for quick revision. Keep it to the few points you would write in the exam.
